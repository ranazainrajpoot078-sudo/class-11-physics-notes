CHAPTER 01

Measurements

Physical quantities, SI units, dimensions and uncertainty

Detailed revision notes

Physical quantity

A physical quantity is a property that can be measured and expressed as a number multiplied by a unit, for example 5 m or 12 s. A complete measurement therefore needs both a numerical value and a unit; a number alone does not describe a physical quantity.

SI base quantities and units

The seven SI base quantities are length (metre, m), mass (kilogram, kg), time (second, s), electric current (ampere, A), thermodynamic temperature (kelvin, K), amount of substance (mole, mol), and luminous intensity (candela, cd). All other SI units are built from these base units.

Derived quantities and units

A derived quantity is defined in terms of base quantities. For example, speed is length divided by time (m s-1), force is mass times acceleration (kg m s-2, called the newton, N), and energy is force times distance (kg m2 s-2, called the joule, J). Pressure is force per unit area (N m-2, called the pascal, Pa).

Dimensions

Dimensions express a quantity in terms of the base quantities mass (M), length (L) and time (T), and where needed current, temperature and amount. For example, velocity has dimensions [L T-1], acceleration [L T-2], force [M L T-2], and energy [M L2 T-2]. Dimensions do not give the numerical value or the unit system, only the kind of quantity.

Dimensional analysis: uses

Dimensional analysis can check whether an equation is dimensionally consistent, convert a unit from one system to another, and help derive a relation between quantities when the form of the relation is not known. In a correct equation, every term added or equated must have the same dimensions.

Dimensional analysis: limitations

Dimensional analysis cannot find pure numbers such as 2, pi or 1/2 in a formula, cannot distinguish quantities with the same dimensions (for example work and torque, or speed and velocity magnitude), and cannot handle equations containing trigonometric, exponential or logarithmic functions of a quantity with dimensions unless the argument is dimensionless.

Accuracy and precision

Accuracy describes closeness to an accepted or true value. Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with one another. A measurement may be precise but not accurate, for example if an instrument has a zero error. A good measurement should be both accurate and precise, and its uncertainty should be stated.

Systematic and random errors

A systematic error shifts all readings in the same direction, for example a zero error, a badly calibrated instrument, or a consistent parallax error. It can often be reduced by calibration or by correcting the reading. A random error varies unpredictably from reading to reading, for example due to small changes in conditions or in judging a scale; it can be reduced by taking many readings and finding the mean.

Absolute and percentage uncertainty

Absolute uncertainty has the same unit as the measurement and states the range within which the true value is expected to lie, for example (2.50 +/- 0.01) m. Percentage uncertainty is the absolute uncertainty divided by the measured value, multiplied by 100%. It is useful for comparing the quality of different measurements.

Combining uncertainties

When quantities are added or subtracted, absolute uncertainties are added. When quantities are multiplied or divided, percentage uncertainties are added. When a quantity is raised to a power n, its percentage uncertainty is multiplied by n. The final result should be quoted to a sensible number of significant figures, with its uncertainty.

Significant figures

Significant figures communicate the precision of a measured value. All non-zero digits are significant, zeros between non-zero digits are significant, and trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant. Leading zeros are not significant. In multiplication and division, the result is quoted to the same number of significant figures as the least precise measurement; in addition and subtraction, it is quoted to the same decimal place as the least precise measurement.

Scientific notation and prefixes

Very large and very small numbers are written in scientific notation, as a number between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of 10, for example 3.0 x 10^8 m s-1. SI prefixes such as kilo (10^3), milli (10^-3), micro (10^-6) and nano (10^-9) are used to avoid long strings of zeros and to keep the numerical value in a convenient range.

Least count and measuring instruments

The least count of an instrument is the smallest value it can measure, for example 1 mm for a metre rule, 0.1 mm for a vernier caliper with a suitable vernier scale, and 0.01 mm for a micrometer screw gauge. A smaller least count gives a more precise reading, but the reading should never be quoted beyond the instrument's least count.

Zero error and parallax

A zero error occurs when an instrument does not read zero when it should, for example when the jaws of a vernier caliper are closed or the spindle of a screw gauge is fully closed. A positive zero error is subtracted from the observed reading and a negative zero error is added. Parallax error occurs when the eye is not directly in line with the scale mark; always read a scale with the eye perpendicular to it.

Orders of magnitude and estimation

An order of magnitude is a power of 10 that gives the approximate size of a quantity. Estimation is useful for checking whether an answer is reasonable: round the numbers, do a rough calculation, and compare the result with the order of magnitude expected. A result that is many orders of magnitude out is almost certainly wrong, even if the arithmetic looks careful.

Solved numerical 1: Percentage uncertainty

Question: A length is measured as (2.50 +/- 0.01) m. Find the percentage uncertainty.

Given: Measured value = 2.50 m; absolute uncertainty = 0.01 m.

Solution: Percentage uncertainty = (0.01 / 2.50) x 100% = 0.40%.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Solved numerical 2: Density and its uncertainty

Question: A block has mass (200 +/- 2) g and volume (50 +/- 1) cm3. Find its density and percentage uncertainty.

Given: Mass = 200 g, uncertainty 1%; volume = 50 cm3, uncertainty 2%.

Solution: Density = mass / volume = 200 / 50 = 4.0 g cm-3. Percentage uncertainty = 1% + 2% = 3%. Absolute uncertainty = 3% of 4.0 = 0.12 g cm-3. Answer: (4.0 +/- 0.12) g cm-3.

For multiplication and division, add percentage uncertainties, not absolute uncertainties.

Solved numerical 3: Checking an equation by dimensions

Question: Check whether s = ut + (1/2) a t^2 is dimensionally correct, where s is displacement, u is initial velocity, a is acceleration and t is time.

Given: [s] = [L]; [u] = [L T-1]; [a] = [L T-2]; [t] = [T].

Solution: [ut] = [L T-1][T] = [L]. [a t^2] = [L T-2][T^2] = [L]. Every term has dimensions [L], so the equation is dimensionally consistent. This check does not prove the numerical factor 1/2 is correct.

Dimensional consistency is necessary for a correct equation, but it is not sufficient on its own.

CHAPTER 02

Force and Motion

Vectors, kinematics, Newton's laws and momentum

Detailed revision notes

Scalars and vectors

A scalar has magnitude only, such as mass, time, speed, distance, energy and temperature. A vector has magnitude and direction, such as displacement, velocity, acceleration and force. Vectors can be resolved into perpendicular components, and the resultant of two vectors is found by vector addition, not by simply adding their magnitudes.

Distance and displacement

Distance is the total path length travelled and is scalar; it is always positive and can only increase along a journey. Displacement is the change in position from start to finish and is vector; its magnitude is the straight-line distance between the two points, and it can be zero even if distance travelled is large, for example on a return journey.

Speed and velocity

Speed is distance divided by time and is scalar. Velocity is displacement divided by time and is vector, so it includes direction. Average speed uses total distance, while average velocity uses total displacement. Instantaneous velocity is the velocity at a particular moment, given by the gradient of a displacement-time graph at that point.

Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, a vector quantity. It can mean a change in speed, a change in direction, or both. Uniform acceleration means velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time intervals. Negative acceleration is often called deceleration when it acts opposite to the direction of motion, but in vector terms it is simply acceleration in the opposite direction.

Motion graphs

In a displacement-time graph, the gradient is velocity and a straight line means constant velocity. In a velocity-time graph, the gradient is acceleration and the area under the graph is displacement. In an acceleration-time graph, the area under the graph is the change in velocity. A curved graph means the gradient, and therefore the quantity it represents, is changing.

Equations of uniformly accelerated motion

For constant acceleration, the equations v = u + at, s = ut + (1/2) a t^2 and v^2 = u^2 + 2as link initial velocity u, final velocity v, acceleration a, time t and displacement s. They apply only when acceleration is constant and motion is in a straight line. A sign convention must be chosen and used consistently, for example taking upward as positive for vertical motion.

Free fall and acceleration due to gravity

Near the Earth's surface, a freely falling body accelerates at about g = 9.8 m s-2 downward, if air resistance is ignored. All bodies fall with the same acceleration in a vacuum, regardless of mass. In air, a light body with a large surface area reaches terminal velocity sooner because air resistance grows with speed until it balances weight.

Newton's first law and inertia

Newton's first law states that a body remains at rest or in uniform motion in a straight line unless acted on by a resultant external force. Inertia is the resistance of a body to a change in its state of motion, and it depends on mass: a larger mass has greater inertia and needs a larger force for the same acceleration.

Newton's second law

Newton's second law states that the resultant force on a body equals the rate of change of its momentum. For constant mass this becomes F = ma, where F is the resultant force, m is mass and a is acceleration in the direction of the force. Force is measured in newtons: 1 N accelerates a 1 kg mass at 1 m s-2.

Newton's third law

Newton's third law states that when one body exerts a force on another, the second body exerts an equal and opposite force on the first. The two forces act on different bodies, are of the same type, and act at the same time. They do not cancel each other in a free-body diagram because they never act on the same body.

Momentum and impulse

Momentum is p = mv, a vector quantity with unit kg m s-1. Impulse is the change in momentum, J = F x delta t, where F is the average force and delta t is the time for which it acts. A longer impact time reduces the average force for the same change in momentum, which is why airbags, padded landing mats and crumple zones increase impact time.

Conservation of momentum

In an isolated system, where the resultant external force is zero, total momentum remains constant. In a collision or explosion, total momentum before equals total momentum after, even though kinetic energy may not be conserved. Elastic collisions conserve both momentum and kinetic energy; inelastic collisions conserve momentum but not kinetic energy, and some energy is transferred to heat, sound or deformation.

Friction

Friction opposes relative motion or attempted motion between surfaces in contact. Static friction prevents motion starting, and kinetic friction acts once sliding has begun. Friction depends on the nature of the surfaces and the normal contact force, but for simple school models it is often treated as independent of the contact area. Friction is useful for walking and braking, but it also causes wear and energy loss.

Projectile motion

Ignoring air resistance, a projectile has zero horizontal acceleration and vertical acceleration g downward. Horizontal and vertical motions are independent: horizontal velocity stays constant, while vertical velocity changes by g each second. The path is a parabola. Time of flight depends only on the vertical motion, while range depends on both launch speed and launch angle, with maximum range at 45 degrees for the same launch and landing level.

Free-body diagrams and resultant force

A free-body diagram shows all the forces acting on one body, drawn as arrows from its centre with correct directions. The resultant force is the vector sum of all forces. If the resultant is zero, the body is in equilibrium: it stays at rest or moves with constant velocity. If the resultant is not zero, the body accelerates in the direction of the resultant, according to F = ma.

Solved numerical 1: Uniform acceleration

Question: A car starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at 3 m s-2 for 4 s. Find its final velocity and displacement.

Given: u = 0, a = 3 m s-2, t = 4 s.

Solution: v = u + at = 0 + 3 x 4 = 12 m s-1. s = ut + (1/2) a t^2 = 0 + (1/2) x 3 x 16 = 24 m.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Solved numerical 2: Impulse and average force

Question: A 0.20 kg ball moving at 10 m s-1 is stopped in 0.05 s. Find the impulse and the average force.

Given: m = 0.20 kg, u = 10 m s-1, v = 0, delta t = 0.05 s.

Solution: Change in momentum = m(v - u) = 0.20 x (0 - 10) = -2.0 kg m s-1, so impulse magnitude is 2.0 N s. Average force = impulse / time = 2.0 / 0.05 = 40 N, opposite to the initial motion.

Impulse is a vector: the sign shows direction, and the magnitude alone is often quoted in practice questions.

Solved numerical 3: Projectile range

Question: A ball is projected at 20 m s-1 at 30 degrees above the horizontal from ground level. Find its time of flight and range, taking g = 10 m s-2.

Given: u = 20 m s-1, angle = 30 degrees, vertical component = 20 sin 30 = 10 m s-1, horizontal component = 20 cos 30 = 17.3 m s-1.

Solution: Time of flight T = 2u sin(theta) / g = 2 x 10 / 10 = 2.0 s. Range R = horizontal velocity x time = 17.3 x 2.0 = 34.6 m.

For the same launch and landing level, the vertical motion determines the time in the air.

CHAPTER 03

Circular and Rotational Motion

Angular motion, centripetal force, torque and angular momentum

Detailed revision notes

Angular displacement

Angular displacement is the angle through which a body turns about an axis, measured in radians. One radian is the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc equal in length to the radius. A full circle is 2 pi radians, which is 360 degrees, so angle in radians = arc length / radius.

Angular velocity and angular acceleration

Angular velocity is the rate of change of angular displacement, measured in rad s-1. Angular acceleration is the rate of change of angular velocity, measured in rad s-2. For a point at distance r from the axis, linear speed v = r x omega and tangential acceleration a = r x alpha, where omega is angular velocity and alpha is angular acceleration.

Uniform circular motion

In uniform circular motion, speed is constant but velocity is not, because direction changes continuously. The body therefore accelerates even though its speed does not change. This acceleration is always directed towards the centre of the circle and is called centripetal acceleration.

Centripetal acceleration and force

Centripetal acceleration is a = v^2 / r = r x omega^2, directed towards the centre. The net inward force needed to produce it is the centripetal force, F = m v^2 / r = m r omega^2. Centripetal force is not a new kind of force; it may be provided by tension, friction, gravity, a normal force, or a combination of forces, depending on the situation.

Centrifugal effect

In a rotating frame of reference, a body appears to be pushed outward; this is called the centrifugal effect. It is not a real force acting on the body in an inertial frame, but a pseudo-force that appears because the rotating frame itself is accelerating. In an inertial frame, the inward centripetal force is the only horizontal force needed to explain circular motion.

Examples of circular motion

A car turning on a flat road needs friction between tyres and road to provide centripetal force. A satellite in circular orbit needs gravity to provide centripetal force. A stone whirled on a string needs tension to provide centripetal force; if the string breaks, the stone flies off tangentially, not radially outward, because no inward force remains.

Banked curves

A road or track can be banked so that part of the normal reaction provides centripetal force, reducing reliance on friction. For a smooth banked curve at the ideal speed, the horizontal component of the normal reaction alone provides the centripetal force, and the vertical component balances weight. At higher or lower speeds, friction is also needed.

Torque

Torque is the turning effect of a force about an axis. Its magnitude is tau = r F sin(theta), where r is the distance from the axis to the point of application, F is the force, and theta is the angle between the position vector and the force. Torque is largest when the force is perpendicular to the position vector and zero when the force acts through the axis or along the position vector.

Rotational form of Newton's second law

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, the resultant torque equals moment of inertia times angular acceleration: tau = I x alpha. This is the rotational analogue of F = ma. A larger moment of inertia means a larger torque is needed for the same angular acceleration.

Moment of inertia

Moment of inertia, I, measures resistance to angular acceleration and depends on the mass of the body and how that mass is distributed about the axis. Mass farther from the axis contributes more to moment of inertia. For a point mass, I = m r^2. For extended bodies, I depends on shape and axis; for example, a solid disc and a hoop of the same mass and radius have different moments of inertia.

Radius of gyration

The radius of gyration, k, is the distance from the axis at which the whole mass of a body could be concentrated as a point mass to give the same moment of inertia, so that I = M k^2, where M is total mass. It gives a simple way to compare how spread out the mass of different bodies is about an axis.

Angular momentum

Angular momentum for a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis is L = I x omega, a vector quantity along the axis of rotation. For a point mass moving in a circle, L = m v r. Angular momentum is conserved when the net external torque is zero, so if moment of inertia decreases, angular velocity increases, and vice versa.

Conservation of angular momentum in action

An ice skater spinning with arms outstretched has a large moment of inertia and a low angular velocity. Pulling the arms in reduces the moment of inertia, so angular velocity increases to keep angular momentum constant, if friction and other external torques are negligible. A similar effect is seen when a diver tucks into a ball during a somersault.

Rotational kinetic energy and work

Rotational kinetic energy is (1/2) I omega^2. Work done by a torque through an angular displacement is torque times angular displacement, and power in rotational motion is torque times angular velocity. A rolling body has both translational kinetic energy, (1/2) m v^2, and rotational kinetic energy, (1/2) I omega^2, so its total kinetic energy is the sum of the two.

Equilibrium of a rigid body

A rigid body is in equilibrium when the resultant force is zero and the resultant torque about any axis is zero. The first condition prevents translational acceleration and the second prevents angular acceleration. A body can be in equilibrium while rotating with constant angular velocity or moving with constant velocity, provided both resultants are zero.

Solved numerical 1: Centripetal force

Question: A 0.5 kg object moves in a circle of radius 2 m at 4 m s-1. Find its centripetal force.

Given: m = 0.5 kg, r = 2 m, v = 4 m s-1.

Solution: F = m v^2 / r = 0.5 x 4^2 / 2 = 0.5 x 16 / 2 = 4 N, directed towards the centre.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Solved numerical 2: Torque

Question: A force of 20 N acts perpendicular to a spanner at a distance of 0.30 m from the nut. Find the torque.

Given: F = 20 N, r = 0.30 m, angle = 90 degrees, so sin(theta) = 1.

Solution: Torque = r F sin(theta) = 0.30 x 20 x 1 = 6.0 N m.

A longer spanner gives a larger torque for the same force, which is why it is easier to undo a tight nut with a long handle.

Solved numerical 3: Angular momentum conservation

Question: A skater rotates at 2.0 rad s-1 with moment of inertia 4.0 kg m2. She pulls her arms in, reducing her moment of inertia to 2.0 kg m2. Find her new angular velocity, assuming no external torque.

Given: I1 = 4.0 kg m2, omega1 = 2.0 rad s-1, I2 = 2.0 kg m2.

Solution: I1 omega1 = I2 omega2, so omega2 = (4.0 x 2.0) / 2.0 = 4.0 rad s-1. Her angular velocity doubles as her moment of inertia halves.

This assumes external torque, including friction, is negligible during the change.

CHAPTER 04

Work, Energy and Power

Work, kinetic energy, potential energy and conservation

Detailed revision notes

Work: the basic idea

Work is energy transferred by a force when the point of application moves. If there is no displacement, no work is done by that force, however tired a person may feel while holding a heavy object still.

Work by a constant force

For a constant force F and displacement s at angle θ between them, W = Fs cosθ. Only the component of force along the displacement does work. The SI unit is the joule (J), equal to one newton-metre.

Positive, negative and zero work

Work is positive when the force component is in the direction of displacement, negative when it opposes displacement, and zero when the force is perpendicular to displacement. Friction acting against motion is a common example of negative work.

Work by a variable force

When force changes with position, work is the area under the force-displacement graph. For a spring stretched by x, the average force is half the final force, which leads to the factor one-half in the stored elastic energy.

Kinetic energy

Kinetic energy is the energy of motion, K = ½mv². It depends on the square of speed, so doubling speed increases kinetic energy four times. It is always positive or zero, and it is a scalar quantity.

Work-energy theorem

The net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, W_net = ΔK. This is useful when the force or acceleration is not constant, provided the net work can be found from force, displacement or energy changes.

Gravitational potential energy

Near Earth's surface, gravitational potential energy is U = mgh, measured from a chosen reference level. Only changes in potential energy matter physically, so the reference level can be chosen for convenience, but it must be stated.

Elastic potential energy

A stretched or compressed ideal spring stores elastic potential energy U = ½kx², where k is the spring constant and x is the extension or compression from its natural length. The energy does not depend on the sign of x.

Conservative and non-conservative forces

Work done by a conservative force, such as gravity or a spring force, depends only on the start and end positions, not on the path. Work done by friction depends on the path, so friction is non-conservative and converts mechanical energy into internal energy.

Conservation of mechanical energy

If only conservative forces do work, total mechanical energy E = K + U remains constant. When friction or air resistance acts, mechanical energy decreases, but total energy, including internal energy, is still conserved.

Power

Power is the rate of doing work or transferring energy. Average power is P_avg = W/Δt. Instantaneous power for a force parallel to velocity is P = Fv. The SI unit is the watt (W), equal to one joule per second.

Efficiency

Efficiency compares useful energy or power output with total energy or power input, usually as a percentage. No real machine is 100% efficient, because some input energy is always transferred to less useful forms, often as heat.

Energy in collisions

In an elastic collision, both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved in the ideal model. In an inelastic collision, momentum is conserved in an isolated system, but some kinetic energy is converted into internal energy, sound or deformation.

Forms and transfer of energy

Energy can appear as kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential, chemical, electrical, thermal or nuclear energy. Work, heating and radiation are ways energy is transferred, not forms stored in a body in the same sense.

Solids and Fluid Dynamics

Elasticity, pressure, flow and Bernoulli's principle

Detailed revision notes

Stress

Stress is the internal force per unit area in a material, stress = F/A. Its SI unit is the pascal (Pa), equal to one newton per square metre. Stress describes how strongly a material is being pulled, pushed or sheared, not the total force alone.

Strain

Strain is the fractional change in a dimension, for example longitudinal strain = change in length / original length. Strain has no unit because it is a ratio of two lengths. A larger strain means a larger relative deformation.

Hooke's law

Within the elastic limit, stress is proportional to strain for many solids. The constant of proportionality is an elastic modulus, which depends on the material and on the type of deformation, not on the size of the particular sample in the same way as stiffness.

Young's modulus

Young's modulus Y is longitudinal stress divided by longitudinal strain. A larger Y means the material stretches less under the same stress, so it is stiffer in tension. Steel has a much larger Young's modulus than rubber.

Bulk and shear moduli

Bulk modulus describes resistance to uniform compression, relating pressure change to fractional volume change. Shear modulus describes resistance to a sideways, shearing deformation. Together with Young's modulus, they describe different ways a solid can deform.

Elastic limit, yield and fracture

Up to the elastic limit, a material returns to its original shape when the load is removed. Beyond the yield point, permanent deformation occurs. Further loading can lead to necking and finally fracture. The stress-strain graph is the key revision diagram for these stages.

Fluid pressure

Pressure is normal force per unit area, P = F/A. In a fluid at rest, pressure acts equally in all directions at a point. Pressure increases with depth because of the weight of fluid above, which is why dams are thicker at the bottom.

Pascal's principle

Pascal's principle states that a pressure change applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of the fluid and to the walls of the container. Hydraulic lifts and brakes use a small force on a small piston to produce a larger force on a larger piston.

Archimedes' principle and buoyancy

A body partly or fully immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of fluid displaced. An object floats when the buoyant force can balance its weight; its average density then determines how much of it stays submerged.

Surface tension

Surface tension arises from cohesive forces between liquid molecules. Molecules at the surface have fewer neighbours than those inside, so the surface behaves like a stretched film. It allows small insects to stand on water and causes droplets to tend toward a spherical shape.

Viscosity

Viscosity is a fluid's resistance to flow, caused by internal friction between layers. Honey is more viscous than water. Viscosity generally decreases with temperature for liquids, which is why warm oil flows more easily than cold oil.

Laminar and turbulent flow

In laminar flow, fluid layers slide smoothly past each other in streamlines, with no mixing between layers. In turbulent flow, the motion becomes irregular, with eddies and mixing. Flow in a pipe is more likely to become turbulent at high speed, in wide pipes, or with low viscosity.

Continuity equation

For steady flow of an incompressible fluid, the volume flow rate is constant along a pipe, so A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. Where the pipe narrows, the fluid must speed up to pass the same volume in the same time.

Bernoulli's principle

For steady, incompressible, non-viscous flow along a streamline, pressure energy, kinetic energy per unit volume and gravitational potential energy per unit volume add to a constant. Where speed increases, pressure generally decreases if height does not change much.

Applications of Bernoulli's principle

Bernoulli's principle helps explain lift on an aerofoil, the Venturi effect in a constricted pipe, and the operation of a Bunsen burner or sprayer. Real applications also involve viscosity and three-dimensional flow, so Bernoulli's equation alone is an idealised model, not a complete explanation of every detail.

Torricelli's theorem

For liquid flowing out of a small hole at depth h below the surface, the exit speed in the ideal model is v = √(2gh), the same speed as a body falling freely through height h. This follows from converting gravitational potential energy into kinetic energy.

CHAPTER 06

Heat and Thermodynamics

Temperature, heat, ideal gases and the first law

Detailed revision notes

Heat and temperature

Temperature describes the thermal state of a body and determines the direction of spontaneous energy transfer. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. A hot body does not contain heat as a stored substance; it has internal energy, and heat is one way that energy moves.

Thermal equilibrium and the zeroth law

Two bodies are in thermal equilibrium when they are at the same temperature and no net heat flows between them. The zeroth law says that if A is in equilibrium with B, and B is in equilibrium with C, then A is in equilibrium with C. This is the basis for temperature measurement with a thermometer.

Temperature scales

The Celsius scale is convenient for everyday use, but gas laws and thermodynamic calculations require absolute temperature in kelvin. The conversion is T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.15. A temperature change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C, but the zero points differ.

Specific heat capacity

Specific heat capacity c is the energy needed to raise the temperature of unit mass by one kelvin without a change of state. Water has a high specific heat capacity, so it warms and cools slowly compared with many metals, which affects coastal climates and cooling systems.

Heat capacity

Heat capacity is the energy needed to raise the temperature of a whole object by one kelvin. It equals mass multiplied by specific heat capacity. Specific heat capacity is a property of the material, while heat capacity depends on how much material is present.

Latent heat

Latent heat is energy absorbed or released during a change of state at constant temperature. Latent heat of fusion is for melting or freezing, and latent heat of vaporisation is for boiling or condensing. During the change, energy changes molecular arrangement rather than average kinetic energy.

Internal energy

Internal energy is the total microscopic kinetic and potential energy of the particles in a system. For an ideal gas, internal energy depends mainly on temperature, because particle interactions are neglected. Internal energy is a state function: it depends on the current state, not on the path taken.

First law of thermodynamics

The first law is energy conservation applied to a thermodynamic system. Using W for work done by the system, ΔU = Q − W. Heat added to a system can increase its internal energy, enable it to do work, or both. Sign conventions must be stated, because some texts define W with the opposite sign.

Work in a thermodynamic process

For a gas expanding against a piston, work done by the gas is force multiplied by displacement, which can be written as pressure multiplied by volume change for constant pressure. On a pressure-volume diagram, work for a process is the area under the curve, so work depends on the path, unlike changes in internal energy.

Isothermal, isobaric, isochoric and adiabatic processes

An isothermal process keeps temperature constant, so for an ideal gas internal energy does not change. An isobaric process keeps pressure constant. An isochoric process keeps volume constant, so no boundary work is done. An adiabatic process has no heat transfer, so any work done comes from a change in internal energy.

Ideal gas equation

The ideal gas equation PV = nRT connects pressure, volume, amount of gas and absolute temperature. It is a good model at low pressure and high temperature, where gas particles are far apart. At high pressure or low temperature, real gases deviate from ideal behaviour.

Kinetic theory of gases

Kinetic theory models a gas as many particles in random motion, colliding elastically with each other and the walls. Pressure arises from particle collisions with the walls. Average translational kinetic energy per particle is proportional to absolute temperature, which links the microscopic model to the measured temperature.

Root mean square speed

Root mean square speed is a typical molecular speed, v_rms = √(3RT/M), where M is molar mass. Lighter molecules move faster than heavier molecules at the same temperature, which is one reason hydrogen escapes from atmospheres more easily than heavier gases.

Thermal expansion

Most solids expand when heated because increased particle vibration increases average separation. Linear expansion, area expansion and volume expansion are related by approximate factors for small changes. Expansion joints in bridges and gaps in railway tracks allow for this effect.

Modes of heat transfer

Conduction transfers energy through particle vibrations and free electrons without bulk movement of the material. Convection transfers energy by bulk movement of a fluid. Radiation transfers energy by electromagnetic waves and needs no medium. Most real situations involve more than one mode.

Heat engines and efficiency

A heat engine takes in heat, does work, and rejects some heat to a colder reservoir. Its efficiency is useful work output divided by heat input. The Carnot efficiency, 1 − Tc/Th with absolute temperatures, is the maximum possible efficiency between those temperatures for a reversible engine, not a value every real engine reaches.

CHAPTER 07

Waves and Vibrations

Simple harmonic motion, wave properties and resonance

Detailed revision notes

Periodic motion

Periodic motion repeats itself after a fixed time interval called the period, T. The number of cycles completed in one second is the frequency, f, measured in hertz (Hz). A vibrating tuning fork, a swinging pendulum and a mass on a spring all show periodic motion, although not all periodic motion is simple harmonic motion.

Simple harmonic motion (SHM)

Simple harmonic motion is a special type of periodic motion in which the acceleration of a body is directly proportional to its displacement from the mean position and is always directed towards that mean position. In symbols, a = −ω²x, where ω is the angular frequency and x is the displacement. The negative sign shows that acceleration always opposes displacement.

Displacement, velocity and acceleration in SHM

If displacement is x = A cos(ωt + φ), where A is amplitude and φ is the initial phase, then velocity is greatest at the mean position and zero at the extreme positions, while acceleration is zero at the mean position and greatest at the extremes. Velocity and acceleration are therefore out of phase with each other, and a velocity–time graph can be used to check whether motion is harmonic.

Amplitude, phase and angular frequency

Amplitude is the maximum displacement from the mean position. Phase describes the state of vibration at an instant, including both displacement and direction of motion. Angular frequency, ω = 2πf = 2π/T, connects the ordinary frequency to the rate at which phase changes, in radians per second.

Energy in SHM

In SHM, energy is continuously exchanged between kinetic energy and potential energy, but the total mechanical energy remains constant if friction and air resistance are neglected. Kinetic energy is maximum at the mean position, potential energy is maximum at the extremes, and total energy is E = ½mω²A². A larger amplitude therefore means a much larger total energy, since energy depends on the square of amplitude.

Mass–spring oscillator

For a mass m attached to a spring of force constant k, the restoring force is F = −kx, which is exactly the condition for SHM. Its period is T = 2π√(m/k), so it does not depend on amplitude for small oscillations. Increasing the mass increases the period, while a stiffer spring decreases it.

Simple pendulum

For a simple pendulum of length l, the period for small angular displacements is T = 2π√(l/g). The period does not depend on the mass of the bob or, for small angles, on the amplitude. This is why a pendulum clock can be regulated by changing the length of the pendulum, and why the same pendulum has a longer period on the Moon, where g is smaller.

Damped oscillations

Damped oscillations are oscillations whose amplitude decreases with time because energy is lost to friction, air resistance or other resistive forces. Light damping allows many oscillations before the motion dies away, while heavy damping stops the motion quickly. A car suspension is designed to be damped so that it does not keep bouncing after a bump.

Forced oscillations and resonance

Forced oscillations occur when an external periodic force drives a system. Resonance occurs when the driving frequency is equal, or very close, to the natural frequency of the system, so the amplitude becomes very large. Resonance can be useful, as in tuning a radio or a musical instrument, but it can also be dangerous, as in a bridge or a building shaken by a periodic force.

Progressive waves

A progressive wave transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter over a long distance. Particles of the medium oscillate about their mean positions as the wave passes. A wave on a rope, a sound wave in air and a light wave in space are all progressive waves, although their media and mechanisms differ.

Transverse and longitudinal waves

In a transverse wave, particles vibrate at right angles to the direction of wave travel, as in a wave on a string or an electromagnetic wave. In a longitudinal wave, particles vibrate parallel to the direction of travel, as in a sound wave, where compressions and rarefactions move through the medium. Only transverse waves can be polarised.

Wavelength, wave speed and frequency

Wavelength, λ, is the distance between two successive points vibrating in phase, such as two successive crests. Wave speed is v = fλ. For a given medium, wave speed is usually constant, so increasing frequency decreases wavelength. The wave equation for a wave travelling in the positive x-direction can be written as y = A sin(kx − ωt), where k = 2π/λ is the wave number.

Superposition and stationary waves

When two or more waves meet, their displacements add by the principle of superposition. If two identical waves travel in opposite directions, they can form a stationary (standing) wave, with nodes where displacement is always zero and antinodes where displacement is maximum. A stretched string fixed at both ends can vibrate in its fundamental mode and in harmonics, with frequencies that are whole-number multiples of the fundamental.

Wave intensity and the Doppler effect

Wave intensity is the power transferred per unit area perpendicular to the direction of travel, and for a wave from a point source it decreases with the square of distance from the source. The Doppler effect is the apparent change in frequency when there is relative motion between source and observer: the frequency appears higher when the source approaches and lower when it recedes. It is used in radar speed checks, astronomy and medical ultrasound.

CHAPTER 08

Physical Optics and Gravitational Waves

Interference, diffraction, polarization and spacetime waves

Detailed revision notes

Wave nature of light

Physical optics studies light using its wave properties, especially superposition, interference, diffraction and polarization. These effects cannot be explained by a simple ray model alone. Light is a transverse electromagnetic wave, so its electric field can vibrate in different directions perpendicular to its direction of travel.

Huygens' principle

Huygens' principle states that every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets, and the new wavefront is the envelope of those wavelets. It explains reflection, refraction and diffraction in a unified way: when a wave passes through a narrow opening, the opening acts like a new point source and the wave spreads out.

Coherent sources

Coherent sources have the same frequency and a constant phase difference. Stable, observable interference patterns require coherence, because if the phase difference changes randomly, bright and dark regions move too quickly to be seen. In Young's experiment, a single source illuminating two slits ensures the two emerging waves are coherent.

Interference

Interference is the result of superposition of two or more waves. Constructive interference occurs when waves arrive in phase, so their amplitudes add and brightness increases. Destructive interference occurs when waves arrive in antiphase, so their amplitudes cancel and brightness decreases. The pattern of alternate bright and dark fringes is direct evidence for the wave nature of light.

Path difference

Path difference is the difference in distance travelled by two waves from their sources to a point. Constructive interference occurs when the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths, mλ. Destructive interference occurs when it is a half-integer number of wavelengths, (m + ½)λ, where m = 0, 1, 2, … . This condition controls the positions of bright and dark fringes.

Young's double-slit experiment

In Young's double-slit experiment, monochromatic light passes through two closely spaced slits and produces equally spaced bright and dark fringes on a distant screen. The fringe spacing is β = λD/d, where λ is wavelength, D is slit-to-screen distance and d is slit separation. Measuring fringe spacing therefore provides a way to measure the wavelength of light.

Diffraction

Diffraction is the spreading of waves when they pass through an opening or around an obstacle whose size is comparable to the wavelength. A single slit produces a broad central maximum with narrower, dimmer side maxima. Diffraction limits the resolving power of telescopes, microscopes and cameras, and it is the reason a sharp shadow is not formed by a very small obstacle.

Diffraction grating

A diffraction grating has many equally spaced slits and produces sharp, bright maxima in directions given by d sinθ = mλ, where d is the slit spacing. Because the maxima are sharp, a grating can separate different wavelengths more clearly than a double slit, and it is used in spectrometers to analyse the colours present in light from stars, lamps and lasers.

Polarization

Polarization is the restriction of the vibrations of a transverse wave to one plane. Unpolarized light, such as sunlight, vibrates in all planes perpendicular to travel. A polarizer transmits only the component vibrating in one direction. Polarization cannot occur for longitudinal waves, so it is strong evidence that light is a transverse wave.

Malus' law and Brewster's angle

Malus' law states that the intensity transmitted by an analyser is I = I₀ cos²θ, where I₀ is the intensity incident on the analyser and θ is the angle between the transmission axes of polarizer and analyser. At Brewster's angle, reflected light is completely polarized, and tanθ_B = n₂/n₁ for light going from a medium of refractive index n₁ to one of index n₂. Polarizing sunglasses reduce glare by blocking horizontally polarized reflected light.

Thin films and colours

Thin films, such as soap bubbles and oil films on water, show colours because light reflected from the top and bottom surfaces interferes. The colour seen depends on film thickness, wavelength and angle of view, because the path difference changes with these factors. This is the same interference principle as in the double slit, applied to reflection rather than transmission.

Gravitational waves

Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime produced by accelerating massive objects, such as two merging black holes or neutron stars. They travel at the speed of light, are transverse, and stretch and squeeze space as they pass. Unlike light, they are not electromagnetic waves and they pass through matter almost undisturbed, so they carry information about events that light cannot reveal directly.

Detecting gravitational waves

Gravitational waves are detected with laser interferometers, such as LIGO, which measure extremely small changes in the lengths of two perpendicular arms. The fractional change, called strain, is h = ΔL/L and can be as small as about 10⁻²¹ for waves from distant mergers. A passing wave makes one arm slightly longer and the other slightly shorter in turn, shifting the interference pattern of the laser light. The first direct detection, in 2015, confirmed a long-standing prediction of general relativity and opened a new way to observe the universe.

Comparing light and gravitational waves

Both light and gravitational waves travel at speed c in vacuum and can be described by wave properties such as wavelength and frequency, but their sources and interactions differ greatly. Light is produced by accelerating charges and interacts strongly with matter, so it can be blocked or absorbed. Gravitational waves are produced by accelerating masses, interact extremely weakly with matter, and therefore require very sensitive detectors and very violent cosmic events to be observed.

CHAPTER 09

Electrostatics and Current Electricity

Charge, electric fields, potential, current and circuits

Detailed revision notes

Electric charge

Electric charge is a property of matter that causes electric forces. There are two types, positive and negative: like charges repel and unlike charges attract. Charge is conserved in an isolated system, meaning it can be transferred but not created or destroyed, and it is quantized, occurring in whole multiples of the elementary charge e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

Coulomb's law

Coulomb's law gives the electrostatic force between two point charges: F = k|q₁q₂|/r², where r is their separation and k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻² in vacuum. The force acts along the line joining the charges, is attractive for opposite charges and repulsive for like charges, and follows an inverse-square law, so doubling the separation reduces the force to one quarter.

Electric field

An electric field is the region around a charge in which another charge experiences a force. Field strength is defined as force per unit positive test charge, E = F/q, and its unit is N C⁻¹, which is equivalent to V m⁻¹. The field due to a point charge is E = kQ/r², directed away from a positive charge and towards a negative charge.

Electric field lines

Electric field lines show the direction a positive test charge would move. They start on positive charges and end on negative charges, never cross, and are closer together where the field is stronger. Between two oppositely charged parallel plates, the field is nearly uniform, so the lines are parallel and equally spaced, except near the edges.

Electric potential and potential difference

Electric potential at a point is the work done per unit positive charge in bringing a test charge from infinity to that point. Potential difference between two points is the work done per unit charge in moving a test charge between them, V = W/q, measured in volts. A positive charge naturally moves from higher to lower potential, while an electron moves in the opposite direction.

Equipotential surfaces

An equipotential surface is a surface on which electric potential is the same at every point. No work is done in moving a charge along an equipotential surface, and electric field lines are always perpendicular to equipotential surfaces. For a point charge, equipotential surfaces are spheres centred on the charge; for a uniform field, they are planes perpendicular to the field.

Capacitors and capacitance

A capacitor stores charge and energy in an electric field between two conductors separated by an insulator. Capacitance is defined as C = Q/V, measured in farads (F). For a parallel-plate capacitor, C = ε₀A/d in vacuum, where A is plate area and d is separation. Inserting a dielectric increases capacitance by the dielectric constant, because it reduces the effective field for the same charge.

Capacitors in series and parallel

In parallel, capacitors have the same potential difference and their capacitances add: C_eq = C₁ + C₂ + … . In series, they have the same charge and reciprocal capacitances add: 1/C_eq = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + … . The energy stored in a capacitor is U = ½QV = ½CV² = Q²/(2C).

Electric current and drift velocity

Electric current is the rate of flow of charge, I = ΔQ/Δt, measured in amperes. In a metal, current is due to free electrons drifting slowly through the lattice, even though the electric signal travels very quickly. Conventional current is defined as the direction of flow of positive charge, opposite to the actual electron drift in a metal wire.

Resistance and resistivity

Resistance is the opposition to current flow. For a uniform wire, R = ρL/A, where ρ is resistivity, L is length and A is cross-sectional area. Resistivity depends on the material and, for most metals, increases with temperature because lattice vibrations scatter electrons more. A long, thin wire therefore has more resistance than a short, thick wire of the same material.

Ohm's law

Ohm's law states that, for a conductor at constant temperature and other constant physical conditions, the current is directly proportional to the potential difference across it: V = IR. Conductors that obey this law, such as a metal wire at constant temperature, are called ohmic. A filament lamp and a diode are non-ohmic, because their resistance changes with current, temperature or direction.

Resistors in series and parallel

In series, the same current flows through each resistor, potential differences add, and R_eq = R₁ + R₂ + … . In parallel, the same potential difference appears across each branch, currents add, and 1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … . A parallel combination always has a smaller equivalent resistance than the smallest individual resistor.

Kirchhoff's laws

Kirchhoff's first law, the junction rule, states that the total current entering a junction equals the total current leaving it, which follows from conservation of charge. Kirchhoff's second law, the loop rule, states that the algebraic sum of potential differences around any closed loop is zero, which follows from conservation of energy. Together, these laws allow currents in complex circuits to be calculated.

Electromotive force and internal resistance

Electromotive force (e.m.f.), ε, is the energy supplied per unit charge by a source, such as a cell or battery. A real cell has internal resistance r, so when current I flows, the terminal potential difference is V = ε − Ir, which is less than the e.m.f. The lost volts, Ir, are the potential drop inside the cell itself.

Electrical power and Joule heating

Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer, P = VI = I²R = V²/R. In a resistor, electrical energy is converted to heat, which is Joule heating. This is useful in heaters and filament lamps, but wasteful in transmission lines, where high voltage and low current are used to reduce I²R losses for a given power delivered.

CHAPTER 10

Electromagnetism

Magnetic fields, force on currents and induction

Detailed revision notes

Magnetic field

A magnetic field is a region in which a magnetic pole, a moving charge, or a current-carrying conductor experiences a magnetic force. Field lines are a visual model: they form closed loops, they never cross, and they are closer together where the field is stronger. Outside a bar magnet they conventionally run from the north pole to the south pole, and inside the magnet they run from south to north to complete the loop.

Magnetic field strength and direction

Magnetic field strength, B, is a vector quantity measured in tesla (T). The direction of the field at a point is the direction of the force on a north pole placed there, or the direction in which a small compass needle points. A uniform field has the same strength and direction everywhere in the region, for example between the flat poles of a strong magnet over a small region.

Magnetic field around a current

A current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field around itself. For a long straight wire the field lines are concentric circles centred on the wire, and their direction follows the right-hand grip rule: point the thumb along the current and the curled fingers give the field direction. The field is stronger close to the wire and weaker farther away.

Field inside a solenoid

A solenoid is a long coil of wire. Inside a long solenoid the magnetic field is strong and approximately uniform, running along the axis of the coil. The field at the ends is about half the field deep inside. Reversing the current reverses the field, and increasing the current or the number of turns per unit length increases the field strength.

Force on a moving charge

A charge moving in a magnetic field experiences a force perpendicular to both its velocity and the magnetic field. The magnitude is F = qvB sinθ, where θ is the angle between velocity and field. The force is zero when the charge moves parallel to the field, and greatest when it moves perpendicular to the field. Because the force is always perpendicular to velocity, it changes direction but not speed, so it does no work on the charge.

Circular motion of a charge in a magnetic field

When a charged particle enters a uniform magnetic field at right angles to the field, the magnetic force acts as a centripetal force and the particle follows a circular path. The radius depends on momentum, charge, and field strength: a faster particle or a weaker field gives a larger circle. If the velocity also has a component along the field, the path becomes a helix.

Force on a current-carrying wire

A current is moving charge, so a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field also experiences a force, F = BIL sinθ, where L is the length of wire in the field and θ is the angle between the wire and the field. The direction is given by Fleming's left-hand rule: first finger for field, second finger for current, thumb for force. This force is the basis of electric motors and loudspeakers.

Force between two parallel currents

Each current produces a magnetic field, and that field exerts a force on the other current. Two parallel wires carrying current in the same direction attract each other, while wires carrying current in opposite directions repel each other. The force is equal and opposite on the two wires, as required by Newton's third law.

Torque on a current loop and the electric motor

A rectangular coil in a magnetic field experiences a torque because forces on opposite sides act in opposite directions. The torque is greatest when the plane of the coil is parallel to the field and zero when it is perpendicular to the field. In a simple DC motor a commutator reverses the current every half-turn so the torque keeps turning the coil in the same direction.

Magnetic flux

Magnetic flux, Φ, measures how much magnetic field passes through a surface. For a flat surface in a uniform field, Φ = BA cosθ, where A is the area and θ is the angle between the field and the normal to the surface. Flux is greatest when the field is perpendicular to the surface and zero when the field lies in the plane of the surface. Its unit is the weber (Wb).

Electromagnetic induction

An electromotive force (emf) is induced in a circuit when the magnetic flux linked with it changes. The change can come from a changing field strength, a changing area, a changing orientation, or motion of a magnet or coil. No flux change means no induced emf, even if a large steady field is present. Induction does not require the circuit to be connected to a battery.

Faraday's law and Lenz's law

Faraday's law states that the magnitude of the induced emf is proportional to the rate of change of flux linkage: ε = N(ΔΦ/Δt) for N turns. Lenz's law gives the direction: the induced current opposes the change that produced it. The minus sign in ε = −N(ΔΦ/Δt) expresses Lenz's law. Opposition here is consistent with energy conservation: work must be done to push a magnet against the opposing induced field.

Generators and transformers

A generator rotates a coil in a magnetic field, so the flux through the coil changes continuously and an alternating emf is induced. A transformer has a primary coil and a secondary coil linked by a changing magnetic flux in a core. It works only with alternating current, because only a changing flux induces an emf in the secondary coil. For an ideal transformer, power in equals power out, so a step-up transformer raises voltage and lowers current.

Eddy currents and back emf

Eddy currents are loops of current induced in a bulk conductor when the flux through it changes. They can cause unwanted heating, so transformer cores are laminated to reduce them, but they are useful in induction heating and magnetic braking. In a motor, the rotating coil also acts as a generator, producing a back emf that opposes the supply voltage; this limits the current when the motor runs fast and explains why a stalled motor draws a large current.

CHAPTER 11

Special Theory of Relativity

Inertial frames, time dilation and mass-energy

Detailed revision notes

Reference frames

A reference frame is a coordinate system together with clocks used to measure where and when events happen. An event is something that happens at one place at one time, such as a light flashing. Different observers may describe the same event with different coordinates, and relativity studies how those descriptions are related.

Inertial and non-inertial frames

An inertial frame is one in which a body free from net force remains at rest or moves in a straight line at constant velocity, so Newton's first law holds. A frame that accelerates or rotates is non-inertial, and fictitious forces appear in it. Special relativity deals with inertial frames moving at constant velocity relative to each other.

First postulate: principle of relativity

The laws of physics have the same form in all inertial frames. No experiment done entirely inside a closed inertial frame can tell whether that frame is at rest or moving uniformly. This extends the familiar idea that mechanical laws are the same in a smoothly moving train and on the ground to all laws of physics, including electromagnetism.

Second postulate: constancy of the speed of light

The speed of light in vacuum, c, is the same for all inertial observers, regardless of the motion of the source or the observer. This is surprising because everyday speeds add in the usual way, but careful experiments found no change in the measured speed of light when the observer's motion changed. Einstein took this result as a postulate.

Relativity of simultaneity

Two events that are simultaneous in one inertial frame need not be simultaneous in another frame moving relative to it. For example, two flashes at opposite ends of a moving train may be judged simultaneous by an observer on the train but not by an observer on the platform. Simultaneity is therefore frame-dependent, not absolute.

Proper time and time dilation

Proper time is the time interval between two events measured by a clock that is present at both events, so the events occur at the same place in that clock's frame. An observer moving relative to that clock measures a longer interval, Δt = γΔt₀, where Δt₀ is the proper time and γ is the Lorentz factor. A moving clock is therefore measured to run slow. The effect is negligible at everyday speeds and becomes large only near the speed of light.

Length contraction

Proper length is the length of an object measured in the frame in which it is at rest. An observer moving relative to the object, along the direction of its length, measures a shorter length, L = L₀/γ. Only the dimension along the direction of motion contracts; dimensions perpendicular to the motion are unchanged. Length contraction is a measurement effect for the moving observer, not a physical squeezing of the object.

Lorentz factor

The Lorentz factor is γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²), where v is the relative speed of the frames. At rest, v = 0 and γ = 1, so classical results are recovered. As v approaches c, the denominator approaches zero and γ grows without limit, which is why no object with mass can be accelerated to the speed of light: it would require infinite energy.

Relativistic momentum and energy

Relativistic momentum is p = γm₀v, where m₀ is rest mass, so momentum grows faster than velocity as speed approaches c. Total energy is E = γm₀c², and kinetic energy is the total energy minus the rest energy: K = (γ − 1)m₀c². At low speeds this reduces to the familiar ½m₀v², which is a useful check on the relativistic formulas.

Mass-energy equivalence

Rest energy is E₀ = m₀c². Mass and energy are two forms of the same quantity: a system that gains energy gains mass, and a system that loses energy loses mass. Because c² is very large, even a small mass change corresponds to a large energy change, which is why nuclear reactions release far more energy per kilogram than chemical reactions.

Space-time and the invariant interval

Special relativity treats space and time together as space-time. While different observers disagree about separate space and time intervals, they agree on a combined space-time interval between two events. This invariance replaces the separate absolutes of Newtonian space and time, and it is the deeper reason why time dilation and length contraction always occur together in a consistent way.

Twin paradox in outline

The twin paradox asks why a travelling twin returns younger than a twin who stays behind, if motion is relative. The resolution is that the situation is not symmetric: the travelling twin must turn around, changing inertial frames, while the stay-at-home twin remains in one inertial frame throughout. The proper time along the travelling twin's path through space-time is shorter, so the travelling twin ages less.

Limits of special relativity

Special relativity applies to inertial frames and does not include gravity or accelerated frames in its basic form; those are treated by general relativity. It also does not replace classical mechanics at everyday speeds: for speeds much smaller than c, γ is extremely close to 1, and Newtonian mechanics is an excellent approximation. Its predictions have been tested with fast particles, atomic clocks on aircraft, and satellite navigation systems.

Question: A clock on a spacecraft measures a proper time interval of 10 s between two events. The spacecraft moves at 0.80c relative to Earth. What time interval is measured on Earth?

Solution: Lorentz factor γ = 1/√(1 − 0.64) = 1/√0.36 = 1/0.60 = 1.667. Dilated time Δt = γΔt₀ = 1.667 × 10 = 16.7 s.

CHAPTER 12

Nuclear and Particle Physics

Nuclei, binding energy, radioactive decay, fission and fusion

Detailed revision notes

Nuclear structure and notation

An atom's nucleus contains protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons, held together by the strong nuclear force. The atomic number Z is the number of protons, and the mass number A is the total number of nucleons, so the neutron number is A − Z. A nuclide is written as ᴬZX, for example ¹⁴₆C has 6 protons and 8 neutrons. Electrons outside the nucleus determine chemical behaviour, while protons determine the element.

Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element, with the same proton number, but different neutron numbers and therefore different mass numbers. They have almost identical chemical properties because chemistry depends mainly on electrons, but different nuclear stability. Some isotopes are stable, while others are radioactive. Isotopes are used in medicine, dating, tracing, and industry, with the choice depending on half-life and type of radiation.

Nuclear size and density

The nucleus is tiny compared with the whole atom: its radius is of order 10⁻¹⁵ m, while atomic size is of order 10⁻¹⁰ m. Nuclear radius increases slowly with mass number, approximately as R = R₀A^(1/3). Because almost all the mass is concentrated in this tiny volume, nuclear density is extremely high, about 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³, and roughly the same for all nuclei.

Mass defect

The mass of a nucleus is less than the sum of the masses of its separate protons and neutrons. This difference is the mass defect, Δm. It arises because energy is released when nucleons bind together, and that energy comes from mass through E = Δmc². The mass defect is therefore a direct measure of how strongly the nucleons are bound.

Binding energy and binding energy per nucleon

Binding energy is the energy needed to separate a nucleus completely into its individual protons and neutrons, equal to Δmc². Binding energy per nucleon is the binding energy divided by the number of nucleons, and it measures nuclear stability per particle. It rises to a maximum near iron and then falls slowly for heavier nuclei. This shape explains why both fission of very heavy nuclei and fusion of very light nuclei can release energy: the products lie closer to the peak.

Alpha decay

In alpha decay an unstable nucleus emits an alpha particle, which is a helium-4 nucleus consisting of 2 protons and 2 neutrons. The parent loses 2 in atomic number and 4 in mass number. Alpha particles are relatively heavy and slow, so they are strongly ionizing but have a short range in air and are stopped by paper or skin. Alpha decay is common in heavy nuclei.

Beta decay

In beta-minus decay a neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton, emitting an electron (the beta particle) and an antineutrino. The atomic number increases by 1 while the mass number stays the same. In beta-plus decay a proton changes into a neutron, emitting a positron and a neutrino. Beta particles are faster and less ionizing than alpha particles, and they penetrate farther, typically stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium.

Gamma radiation

Gamma radiation is high-energy electromagnetic radiation emitted when an excited nucleus drops to a lower energy state, often after alpha or beta decay. Gamma photons have no charge and no rest mass, so they are weakly ionizing per interaction but highly penetrating, requiring thick lead or concrete for shielding. Gamma emission changes neither atomic number nor mass number.

Radioactive decay law

Radioactive decay is spontaneous and random for an individual nucleus: it cannot be predicted when a particular nucleus will decay, and it is not affected by temperature, pressure, or chemical state. For a large sample, however, the number of undecayed nuclei falls exponentially, N = N₀e^(−λt), where λ is the decay constant. Activity, A = λN, is the number of decays per second, measured in becquerel (Bq), where 1 Bq = 1 decay per second.

Half-life

Half-life, T½, is the time for the number of undecayed nuclei, or the activity, to fall to half its initial value. After one half-life half remains, after two half-lives one quarter remains, and after three half-lives one eighth remains. Half-life is related to the decay constant by λ = ln2 / T½. Different isotopes have vastly different half-lives, from fractions of a second to billions of years, which determines their uses and hazards.

Nuclear fission

Fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus, such as uranium-235, into two lighter nuclei, usually after absorbing a slow neutron. The process releases energy and typically two or three neutrons, which can cause further fissions in a chain reaction. In a reactor the chain reaction is controlled with control rods and a moderator; in an uncontrolled chain reaction the energy is released very rapidly. Fission energy comes from the increase in binding energy per nucleon of the products.

Nuclear fusion

Fusion joins light nuclei, such as hydrogen isotopes, to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy when the product has higher binding energy per nucleon. Fusion requires very high temperatures so that nuclei can overcome their electrical repulsion and come close enough for the strong force to act. It powers the Sun and stars, and it is the hoped-for basis of future fusion power, but sustaining controlled fusion on Earth remains technically difficult.

Elementary particles in outline

Protons and neutrons are not fundamental: each is made of three quarks held together by gluons. Leptons, such as electrons and neutrinos, are fundamental particles that are not made of quarks. Every particle has a corresponding antiparticle with the same mass but opposite charge and other quantum numbers; when a particle meets its antiparticle they can annihilate, converting mass into energy, usually as photons.

Radiation safety and uses

Ionizing radiation can damage cells and DNA, so exposure is kept as low as reasonably achievable using time, distance, and shielding: spend less time near a source, stay farther away, and use appropriate shielding for the radiation type. The same properties make radioactivity useful: tracers in medicine, sterilization, smoke detectors, thickness gauges, carbon dating of once-living material, and cancer treatment all rely on controlled use of radioactive isotopes.
