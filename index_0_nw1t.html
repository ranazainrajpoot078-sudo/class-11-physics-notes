CHAPTER 01

Measurements

Physical quantities, SI units, dimensions and uncertainty

Detailed revision notes

Physical quantity

A physical quantity is a property that can be measured and expressed as a number multiplied by a unit, for example 5 m or 12 s.

SI system

The seven SI base quantities are length (m), mass (kg), time (s), electric current (A), thermodynamic temperature (K), amount of substance (mol), and luminous intensity (cd). Derived units are formed from base units; for example, force is measured in kg m s⁻², called the newton.

Accuracy and precision

Accuracy describes closeness to an accepted value. Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with one another. A measurement may be precise but not accurate.

Dimensions

Dimensions express a quantity in terms of base quantities. For example, velocity has dimensions [LT⁻¹], acceleration [LT⁻²], force [MLT⁻²], and energy [ML²T⁻²].

Significant figures

Significant figures communicate the precision of a measured value. Leading zeros are not significant; zeros between non-zero digits are significant. Avoid reporting a calculated result with unjustified precision.

Uncertainty

Every measurement has uncertainty due to instrument resolution, technique and environmental conditions. Absolute uncertainty has the same unit as the measurement; percentage uncertainty is the relative uncertainty multiplied by 100%.

Solved numerical

Question: A length is measured as (2.50 ± 0.01) m. Find percentage uncertainty.

Solution: Percentage uncertainty = (0.01 / 2.50) × 100% = 0.40%.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Important formulas

Percentage uncertainty = (absolute uncertainty / measured value) × 100%
Density: ρ = m/V; dimensions [ML⁻³]
Velocity: [LT⁻¹]; acceleration: [LT⁻²]; force: [MLT⁻²]

Check each formula's assumptions, symbols and units against the prescribed textbook.

CHAPTER 02

Force and Motion

Vectors, kinematics, Newton's laws and momentum

Detailed revision notes

Scalars and vectors

A scalar has magnitude only, such as mass, time or speed. A vector has magnitude and direction, such as displacement, velocity, acceleration and force. Vectors can be resolved into perpendicular components.

Displacement, speed and velocity

Distance is the total path length and is scalar. Displacement is the change in position and is vector. Average speed is total distance divided by time; average velocity is displacement divided by time.

Uniform acceleration

For constant acceleration, velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time intervals. The standard equations apply only when acceleration is constant.

Newton's laws

The first law describes inertia. The second law states that net force equals the rate of change of momentum; for constant mass, F = ma. The third law says interacting bodies exert equal and opposite forces on each other.

Momentum and impulse

Momentum is p = mv. Impulse equals the change in momentum. In an isolated system, total momentum remains constant if the net external force is zero.

Projectile motion

Ignoring air resistance, horizontal acceleration is zero and vertical acceleration is g downward. Horizontal and vertical motions can be analysed separately.

Solved numerical

Question: A car starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at 3 m s⁻² for 4 s. Find final velocity and displacement.

Solution: Given u = 0, a = 3 m s⁻², t = 4 s. v = u + at = 12 m s⁻¹. s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½(3)(16) = 24 m.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Important formulas

v = u + at
s = ut + ½at²
v² = u² + 2as
F = ma; p = mv; impulse J = FΔt = Δp
For a projectile launched at speed u and angle θ: T = 2u sinθ/g; H = u² sin²θ/(2g); R = u² sin2θ/g (same launch and landing level)

CHAPTER 03

Circular and Rotational Motion

Angular motion, centripetal force, torque and angular momentum

Detailed revision notes

Angular quantities

Angular displacement is measured in radians. Angular velocity is rate of change of angular displacement and angular acceleration is rate of change of angular velocity.

Centripetal acceleration

An object moving in a circle has acceleration toward the centre even if its speed is constant, because its velocity direction changes.

Centripetal force

The net inward force needed for circular motion is F = mv²/r = mrω². It is not a new kind of force; tension, friction or gravity may provide it.

Torque

Torque is the turning effect of a force. Its magnitude is τ = rF sinθ, where r is the distance from the axis and θ is the angle between position and force vectors.

Moment of inertia

Moment of inertia I measures resistance to angular acceleration and depends on mass distribution and the selected axis. Rotational dynamics follows τ = Iα for a rigid body about a fixed axis.

Angular momentum

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, L = Iω. Angular momentum is conserved when the net external torque is zero.

Solved numerical

Question: A 0.5 kg object moves in a circle of radius 2 m at 4 m s⁻¹. Find its centripetal force.

Solution: F = mv²/r = 0.5 × 4² / 2 = 4 N, directed toward the centre.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Important formulas

v = rω
aᶜ = v²/r = rω²
Fᶜ = mv²/r = mrω²
τ = rF sinθ; τ = Iα
L = Iω; rotational kinetic energy = ½Iω²

Check each formula's assumptions, symbols and units against the prescribed textbook.

CHAPTER 04

Work, Energy and Power

Work, kinetic energy, potential energy and conservation

Detailed revision notes

Work

Work is done by a force when displacement has a component along the force. Work is positive when force has a component along displacement, negative when opposite, and zero when perpendicular.

Kinetic energy

Kinetic energy is the energy associated with motion. The work-energy theorem states that net work equals the change in kinetic energy.

Potential energy

Gravitational potential energy near Earth's surface is mgh relative to a chosen reference level. Elastic potential energy in an ideal spring is ½kx².

Conservation of mechanical energy

When only conservative forces do work, the sum of kinetic and potential energies remains constant. Friction transfers mechanical energy into internal energy.

Power

Power is the rate of doing work or transferring energy. Average power is work divided by elapsed time; instantaneous power for a force is the dot product of force and velocity.

Solved numerical

Question: A 10 N force moves a box 3 m in the direction of the force. Find the work done.

Solution: W = Fs cos 0° = 10 × 3 × 1 = 30 J.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Important formulas

W = Fs cosθ
K = ½mv²; near Earth U = mgh
Spring energy Uₛ = ½kx²
W_net = ΔK
P_avg = W/Δt; P = Fv when force and velocity are parallel

Check each formula's assumptions, symbols and units against the prescribed textbook.

CHAPTER 05

Solids and Fluid Dynamics

Elasticity, pressure, flow and Bernoulli's principle

Detailed revision notes

Stress and strain

Stress is force per unit area. Strain is the fractional change in dimension and has no unit. Within the elastic limit, stress is proportional to strain for many materials.

Elastic moduli

Young's modulus is longitudinal stress divided by longitudinal strain. A larger modulus means a material is stiffer under the specified loading conditions.

Fluid pressure

Pressure is normal force per unit area. In a stationary fluid, pressure increases with depth because of the weight of fluid above.

Continuity equation

For steady incompressible flow, the volume flow rate is constant, so A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. Fluid speed rises in a narrower section of pipe.

Bernoulli's principle

For ideal, incompressible, non-viscous steady flow along a streamline, pressure energy, kinetic energy per unit volume and gravitational potential energy per unit volume sum to a constant.

Viscosity

Viscosity describes a fluid's resistance to flow. Real fluids lose mechanical energy through viscous effects, so Bernoulli's ideal equation may not apply exactly.

Solved numerical

Question: Water pressure increases with depth. Calculate the gauge pressure 2 m below the surface, taking ρ = 1000 kg m⁻³ and g = 9.8 m s⁻².

Solution: ΔP = ρgh = 1000 × 9.8 × 2 = 19,600 Pa = 19.6 kPa.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Important formulas

Stress = F/A; strain = ΔL/L
Young's modulus Y = (F/A)/(ΔL/L)
Pressure P = F/A; hydrostatic pressure difference ΔP = ρgh
Continuity: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂
Bernoulli: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant (ideal flow)

Check each formula's assumptions, symbols and units against the prescribed textbook.

CHAPTER 06

Heat and Thermodynamics

Temperature, heat, ideal gases and the first law

Detailed revision notes

Heat and temperature

Temperature describes thermal state and determines the direction of spontaneous thermal energy transfer. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.

Specific heat capacity

Specific heat capacity is the energy required to raise the temperature of unit mass of a substance by one kelvin, without a change of state.

Internal energy

Internal energy is the microscopic kinetic and potential energy of particles in a system. It can change through heat transfer or work.

First law

Using W as work done by the system, the first law is ΔU = Q − W. Energy supplied as heat may increase internal energy and/or be used to do work.

Thermodynamic processes

In an isothermal process temperature stays constant; in an isochoric process volume stays constant; in an isobaric process pressure stays constant; an adiabatic process has no heat transfer.

Ideal gas

The ideal gas equation connects pressure, volume, amount of gas and absolute temperature. Temperature must be expressed in kelvin.

Solved numerical

Question: How much heat is needed to raise 0.5 kg of water by 10 K? Take c = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Solution: Q = mcΔT = 0.5 × 4200 × 10 = 21,000 J = 21 kJ.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Important formulas

Q = mcΔT (no phase change)
PV = nRT
ΔU = Q − W, where W is work done by the system
Kelvin temperature: T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.15

Check each formula's assumptions, symbols and units against the prescribed textbook.

CHAPTER 07

Waves and Vibrations

Simple harmonic motion, wave properties and resonance

Detailed revision notes

Periodic motion

Periodic motion repeats after a fixed time interval called the period. Frequency is the number of cycles per second and is measured in hertz.

Simple harmonic motion

In SHM, acceleration is proportional to displacement from equilibrium and directed towards equilibrium. It can be described by x = A cos(ωt + φ).

Mass-spring oscillator

For an ideal spring-mass system, the period depends on mass and spring constant. Increasing mass increases period; increasing spring stiffness decreases it.

Wave motion

A wave transfers energy and information through a medium or field. In a mechanical wave, particles oscillate about equilibrium rather than travelling with the wave over long distances.

Wave properties

Wavelength is the distance between successive points in phase. Wave speed equals frequency multiplied by wavelength.

Resonance

Resonance occurs when a periodic driving force is near the natural frequency of a system, producing a large response when damping is small.

Solved numerical

Question: A wave has frequency 50 Hz and wavelength 0.8 m. Find its speed.

Solution: v = fλ = 50 × 0.8 = 40 m s⁻¹.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Important formulas

f = 1/T; ω = 2πf
Wave speed v = fλ
SHM acceleration a = −ω²x
Spring-mass period T = 2π√(m/k)
Simple pendulum (small angle): T = 2π√(l/g)

Check each formula's assumptions, symbols and units against the prescribed textbook.

CHAPTER 08

Physical Optics and Gravitational Waves

Interference, diffraction, polarization and spacetime waves

Detailed revision notes

Wave nature of light

Physical optics studies light using its wave properties. Superposition explains interference and diffraction patterns.

Coherent sources

Coherent sources have the same frequency and a constant phase difference. Stable interference patterns require coherence and suitable overlap.

Interference

Constructive interference occurs when waves arrive in phase; destructive interference occurs when they arrive out of phase. Path difference determines the phase relation.

Young's double-slit experiment

Two narrow coherent slits produce alternating bright and dark fringes on a screen. For small angles, fringe spacing depends on wavelength, screen distance and slit separation.

Diffraction and polarization

Diffraction is spreading around an aperture or obstacle and is significant when dimensions are comparable to wavelength. Polarization shows that light is a transverse wave.

Gravitational waves

Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime produced by accelerating asymmetric mass distributions, such as orbiting compact objects. They are distinct from ordinary sound or electromagnetic waves.

Solved numerical

Question: In a double-slit experiment, λ = 600 nm, D = 2 m and d = 0.5 mm. Find fringe spacing.

Solution: β = λD/d = (600 × 10⁻⁹ × 2)/(0.5 × 10⁻³) = 2.4 × 10⁻³ m = 2.4 mm.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Important formulas

Young's double-slit fringe spacing: β = λD/d (small-angle approximation)
Bright fringes: path difference = nλ
Dark fringes: path difference = (n + ½)λ
For small angles, sinθ ≈ tanθ ≈ θ (radians)

Check each formula's assumptions, symbols and units against the prescribed textbook.

CHAPTER 09

Electrostatics and Current Electricity

Charge, electric fields, potential, current and circuits

Detailed revision notes

Electric charge

Electric charge is a property of matter. Like charges repel and unlike charges attract. Charge is conserved and quantized in units of the elementary charge.

Coulomb's law

The electrostatic force between two point charges in vacuum is proportional to the product of their magnitudes and inversely proportional to the square of separation.

Electric field and potential

Electric field is force per unit positive test charge. Electric potential difference is work done per unit charge to move a test charge between points, with sign depending on the chosen direction.

Current and resistance

Current is charge passing a cross-section per unit time. Resistance relates potential difference to current for an ohmic conductor under constant physical conditions.

Series and parallel circuits

In series, the same current passes through each resistor and resistances add. In parallel, the same potential difference appears across each branch and reciprocal resistances add.

Electrical power

Electrical power is the rate of electrical energy transfer. The formula used depends on the circuit relationship and assumptions.

Solved numerical

Question: A 6 Ω resistor is connected to a 12 V supply. Find current and power.

Solution: I = V/R = 12/6 = 2 A. P = VI = 12 × 2 = 24 W.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Important formulas

Coulomb's law: F = k|q₁q₂|/r²
Electric field: E = F/q; potential difference V = W/q
I = ΔQ/Δt; Ohm's law V = IR
Series: R_eq = R₁ + R₂ + …
Parallel: 1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …
P = VI = I²R = V²/R

Check each formula's assumptions, symbols and units against the prescribed textbook.

CHAPTER 10

Electromagnetism

Magnetic fields, force on currents and induction

Detailed revision notes

Magnetic fields

A magnetic field describes magnetic influence in a region. Field lines form closed loops; outside a bar magnet they conventionally run from north to south.

Force on a moving charge

A moving charge in a magnetic field experiences a force perpendicular to its velocity and the field. The force is zero when motion is parallel to the field.

Force on a current-carrying wire

A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force whose direction depends on current and field directions.

Magnetic flux

Magnetic flux through a flat surface in a uniform field depends on magnetic field strength, area and the angle between the field and the surface normal.

Electromagnetic induction

An emf is induced when magnetic flux linked with a circuit changes. Faraday's law gives its magnitude and Lenz's law its direction.

Applications

Generators use electromagnetic induction to convert mechanical energy into electrical energy. Transformers use changing magnetic flux to change alternating voltage.

Solved numerical

Question: A 0.2 m wire carries 3 A perpendicular to a 0.5 T field. Find the magnetic force.

Solution: F = BIL sin90° = 0.5 × 3 × 0.2 = 0.30 N.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Important formulas

Magnetic force on charge: F = |q|vB sinθ
Force on wire: F = BIL sinθ
Magnetic flux: Φ = BA cosθ
Faraday's law: ε = −N(ΔΦ/Δt)
Ideal transformer: Vₛ/Vₚ = Nₛ/Nₚ

Check each formula's assumptions, symbols and units against the prescribed textbook.

CHAPTER 11

Special Theory of Relativity

Inertial frames, time dilation and mass-energy

Detailed revision notes

Reference frames

A reference frame is a coordinate system and clock arrangement used to describe events. An inertial frame is one in which a free body remains at rest or moves uniformly in a straight line.

Two postulates

The laws of physics have the same form in all inertial frames, and the speed of light in vacuum is the same for all inertial observers, independent of source motion.

Relativity of simultaneity

Events simultaneous in one inertial frame need not be simultaneous in another frame moving relative to the first.

Time dilation

A moving clock is measured to run slower relative to an observer when comparing the appropriate events. The proper time is measured in the frame where the two events occur at the same place.

Length contraction

The length of an object along the direction of relative motion is measured shorter in a frame where the object is moving than its proper length.

Mass-energy equivalence

Rest energy is E₀ = mc². A small mass change corresponds to a large energy change because c² is very large.

Solved numerical

Question: An object has rest mass 2 kg. Calculate its rest energy using c = 3 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

Solution: E₀ = mc² = 2 × (3 × 10⁸)² = 1.8 × 10¹⁷ J.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Important formulas

Lorentz factor: γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²)
Time dilation: Δt = γΔt₀
Length contraction: L = L₀/γ (along direction of motion)
Rest energy: E₀ = mc²; c ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹

Check each formula's assumptions, symbols and units against the prescribed textbook.

CHAPTER 12

Nuclear and Particle Physics

Nuclei, binding energy, radioactive decay, fission and fusion

Detailed revision notes

Nuclear structure

An atomic nucleus contains protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons. The atomic number Z is the proton number and mass number A is the total number of nucleons.

Mass defect and binding energy

The mass of a bound nucleus is less than the sum of the masses of its separated nucleons. The mass defect corresponds to binding energy through E = Δmc².

Radioactivity

Unstable nuclei may emit alpha particles, beta particles or gamma radiation. Radioactive decay is spontaneous and random for an individual nucleus.

Half-life

Half-life is the time for the number of undecayed nuclei, or activity, to fall to half its initial value. Different isotopes have different half-lives.

Fission and fusion

Fission splits a heavy nucleus into lighter nuclei; fusion joins light nuclei. Both can release energy when the products have greater binding energy per nucleon.

Radiation safety

Ionizing radiation can damage living tissue. Exposure is managed through time, distance and shielding, following appropriate safety rules.

Solved numerical

Question: A sample starts with 800 radioactive nuclei. How many remain after three half-lives?

Solution: N = 800 × (1/2)³ = 800/8 = 100 nuclei.

Always write given data, formula, substitution, calculation and final answer with unit.

Important formulas

Mass-energy: E = Δmc²
Radioactive decay: N = N₀(½)^(t/T½)
Activity: A = λN
Decay constant and half-life: λ = ln2/T½
Nuclear notation: ᴬ_ZX, where A = Z + neutron number

Check each formula's assumptions, symbols and units against the prescribed textbook.

Quick formula reference

This is a quick revision list. Open the chapter sections for context and conditions.

Motion: v = u + at; s = ut + ½at²; v² = u² + 2as
Momentum: p = mv; impulse = Δp
Circular motion: v = rω; aᶜ = v²/r
Work and energy: W = Fs cosθ; K = ½mv²; U = mgh
Fluids: P = F/A; ΔP = ρgh; A₁v₁ = A₂v₂
Heat: Q = mcΔT; PV = nRT; ΔU = Q − W
Waves: v = fλ; f = 1/T
Electricity: V = IR; P = VI; F = k|q₁q₂|/r²
Induction: ε = −NΔΦ/Δt
Relativity/nuclear: γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²); E = mc²
